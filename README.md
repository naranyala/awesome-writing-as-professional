# Awesome Writing as a Professional 

> A comprehensive catalog of writing discipline, craft, systems, and practice for professionals — because every serious job is a writing job.

Writing is not a "soft skill." It is the primary interface through which modern work gets decided, delegated, documented, and sold. This catalog treats writing as a **professional discipline**: something you train, measure, and maintain like any other craft — with frameworks, templates, tools, and career paths.

## Why Writing Is a Professional Discipline

- Writing is **thinking made visible** — unclear writing usually signals unclear thinking.
- In async, remote, and global teams, the written word is the default record of decisions.
- Promotion, persuasion, and reputation all compound through what you write over time.
- Every document is leverage: one good memo can align a hundred people.
- Writing survives you: docs, decisions, and postmortems outlast any meeting.
- Career mobility follows writing: the people who can explain work get to lead work.

## Skill Paths

Structured 30–90 day paths by level and goal.

### Beginner (First 30 Days)

- Goal: write clear, short workplace messages and simple docs.
- Week 1: Master email and async message structure (see [Genre Deep Dives](#genre-deep-dives)).
- Week 2: Practice active voice and cutting 20% from every draft.
- Week 3: Write one process doc (SOP) for something you own.
- Week 4: Collect feedback from three readers on clarity.
- Resources: *On Writing Well*, Microsoft Writing Style Guide, Purdue OWL.

### Intermediate (30–90 Days)

- Goal: own your role's core genres with structure and speed.
- Learn the Pyramid Principle for recommendations and memos.
- Master one technical doc type (RFC, PRD, postmortem, or brief).
- Build a personal style sheet and reusable template library.
- Publish one external piece (blog, docs contribution, or talk) per month.
- Resources: *The Pyramid Principle*, Google Tech Writing courses, Diátaxis.

### Advanced (90+ Days)

- Goal: influence through writing systems, not single documents.
- Design content systems (style guides, templates, editorial review).
- Write strategy docs and long-form narrative that move organizations.
- Mentor others; run writing reviews for your team.
- Resources: *Docs Like Code*, voice/tone system case studies, conference talks.

## The Writing Discipline

Principles and habits that separate occasional writers from professionals.

### Principles

- **Write for the reader, not yourself.** Start from their question, not your process.
- **Clarity over cleverness.** If a sentence needs re-reading, rewrite it.
- **One document, one job.** Decide the single outcome each piece must produce.
- **Structure is half the writing.** Outline before you draft; revise structure before prose.
- **Write short.** Cut every word that does not change the reader's next action.
- **Respect attention.** Frontload conclusions; bury nothing important in paragraph seven.
- **Assume busy readers.** Most people scan first; design for scanning, reward reading.
- **Make the ask explicit.** Every doc should answer: what do you want from the reader?

### Daily and Weekly Habits

- Daily writing minutes (even 30) beat occasional marathon sessions.
- Keep a commonplaces notebook: capture phrases, structures, and arguments worth reusing.
- Re-read what you shipped last week and mark one thing to improve next time.
- Maintain a personal style sheet (terms, capitalization, preferred phrasings).
- Ship something weekly: memo, post, docs update, or email that could have been a meeting.
- Protect a daily "deep writing" block separate from chat and email.

### Craft Routines

- Reverse outline: after drafting, outline what you actually wrote and fix the gaps.
- Read aloud: your ear catches what your eye forgives.
- Cut 10%: the second draft is usually shorter and stronger.
- Timebox drafting vs. editing — do not polish paragraph one while the outline is still broken.
- Write the executive summary last; verify the body actually supports it.

### Thinking Before Writing

- Answer four questions first: *Who is this for? What do they need to decide or do? What is the one key message? What is the right form (email, doc, brief)?*
- Sketch a one-sentence thesis. If you cannot state it, you are not ready to draft.
- List the three supporting points before writing paragraphs.

## Genre Deep Dives

Practical patterns for the documents professionals write most.

### Email and Async Messages

- Subject line = the ask or decision, not "Quick question."
- Structure: context (1–2 lines) → ask → deadline → background link.
- One email, one topic. Split threads that branch.
- Use numbered options when you need a decision: "Approve A, B, or C."
- BCC vs CC: CC people who need visibility; BCC only for protection from reply-all.
- When the thread hits 5+ people or 3+ back-and-forths, write a summary doc and link it.

**Subject line patterns:**
- `[Decision needed] Approve Q3 hiring plan by Fri`
- `[FYI] Org restructure — what changes for your team`
- `[Action] Review draft RFC before Thursday`

### Chat and Slack

- Default to async. Reserve synchronous chat for unblocking, not deciding.
- Post decisions back into a durable doc; chat is ephemeral.
- Use threads; avoid channel sprawl for multi-topic discussions.
- For complex asks, write a short doc and link it with a 3-sentence summary.
- Escalation ladder: chat → doc → meeting. Never reverse it for complex topics.

### Meeting Documentation

**Pre-read (before meeting):**
- Decision to make, options with trade-offs, recommended path, open questions.
- Send 24+ hours early; assume not everyone reads it — design for partial attention.

**Agenda:**
- Outcome per item ("Decide X", "Align on Y", "Inform on Z") with timeboxes.

**Minutes and decision log:**
- Date, attendees, decisions (with owner), action items (with owner and date), parking lot.
- Publish within 24 hours while memory is fresh.

### Status Reporting

- Lead with status: On track / At risk / Off track — then the why.
- Structure: headline → progress since last update → risks/blockers → asks → next.
- Numbers without narrative are noise; narrative without numbers is vibes.
- Write for people who will not read every line: put deltas and asks up top.
- Keep a "decision log" section over time so readers can reconstruct history.

### Proposals and Executive Briefs

Use the Pyramid Principle:

1. **Situation** — the stable, agreed context.
2. **Complication** — what changed or threatens the situation.
3. **Question** — the decision or problem that follows.
4. **Answer** — your recommendation, stated first.
5. **Supporting arguments** — three (max) reasons, each with evidence.
6. **Next steps** — owners, dates, and what you need from the reader.

Executive brief checklist:
- [ ] One-page length (or clearly justified longer form)
- [ ] Recommendation in the first paragraph
- [ ] Trade-offs named honestly
- [ ] Cost/effort/risk roughly estimated
- [ ] Explicit ask of the reader

### Technical Design Docs and RFCs

Common structure (adapt to your org):

1. **Summary** — what you propose, in three sentences.
2. **Motivation / problem** — why now, who is hurt without it.
3. **Goals and non-goals** — scope boundaries.
4. **Proposed design** — the core approach with diagrams.
5. **Alternatives considered** — at least two, with why they lost.
6. **Risks and open questions** — intellectual honesty builds trust.
7. **Implementation plan** — phases, owners, rollback.
8. **References** — links to prior art and related docs.

RFC hygiene:
- Name a reviewer set and a decision deadline up front.
- Separate blocking feedback from non-blocking nits.
- Record the final decision and rationale in the doc, not only in Slack.

### Architecture Decision Records (ADRs)

Lightweight, immutable decision logs. Typical template:

- **Title:** ADR-NNN: Decision title
- **Status:** Proposed | Accepted | Superseded by ADR-XXX
- **Context:** Forces and constraints at decision time.
- **Decision:** What we chose.
- **Consequences:** What becomes easier, harder, or irreversible.

Resources:
- [ADR GitHub organization](https://github.com/adr/) — ADR tools and examples
- [Michael Nygard — Documenting Architecture Decisions](https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions) — the original ADR article

### Product Requirement Documents (PRDs)

- **Problem / user need** — with evidence (research, data, support tickets).
- **Success metrics** — how you will know it worked.
- **Requirements** — must/should/will-not, prioritized.
- **User stories or jobs-to-be-done** — as appropriate for your team.
- **Open questions / risks** — explicit, owned.
- **Launch plan pointer** — link, do not duplicate the launch doc.

### Policies and SOPs

- Audience and applicability first ("This applies to all engineers on-call").
- Numbered steps for procedures; tables for rules and exceptions.
- Owner and review cadence visible at the top ("Owner: Security; Review: semi-annual").
- Version and changelog so people trust the document.
- Define escalation paths and exception processes — policies without escape hatches get ignored.

### Postmortems and Incident Reports

Blameless by design. Focus on systems, not people.

- **Summary** — what happened, in two sentences.
- **Impact** — users, services, revenue, duration, severity.
- **Timeline** — UTC timestamps, who did what, links to evidence.
- **Root cause** — proximate trigger plus contributing factors.
- **What went well / poorly** — honest, specific.
- **Action items** — owned, dated, and tracked to completion.

Templates:
- [PagerDuty postmortem template](https://github.com/PagerDuty/incident-response-docs/blob/master/docs/after/post_mortem_template.md)
- [dastergon/postmortem-templates](https://github.com/dastergon/postmortem-templates) — collection of templates
- [Google SRE postmortem culture](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/)

### Knowledge Base and Help Content

- Title = the user's task or question ("Reset your password").
- Lead with the solution; put context below.
- One task per article; link related tasks.
- Include screenshots or short videos only when steps are non-obvious.
- Add "Last updated" dates and an owner contact for trust.
- Measure success by task completion and deflection, not pageviews.

## Code-Adjacent Writing

Engineers write constantly, even when they do not call it writing.

### Commit Messages

- Imperative mood subject: "Fix null check in auth middleware"
- Wrap body at ~72 characters; explain *why*, not just *what*.
- Reference issue IDs; note breaking changes prominently.

### Pull Request Descriptions

- What changed and why
- How to test/reproduce
- Screenshots or recordings for UI
- Risk and rollback plan
- Reviewer guidance (what to focus on)

### Code Comments and Docstrings

- Comments explain intent and constraints, not restatement of syntax.
- Delete comments that lie; stale comments are worse than none.
- Docstrings describe inputs, outputs, errors, and side effects.

### README Files

- What the project is, who it is for, how to install and run in under five minutes.
- Badges, screenshots, and examples reduce support load.
- Link to deeper docs; do not paste the entire manual in the README.

Resources:
- [ottekkat/awesome-readme](https://github.com/ottekkat/awesome-readme)
- [Google Tech Writing for Engineers](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing)

## Document Templates and Checklists

Copy-adapt these into your team's wiki or docs repo.

### Universal Doc Skeleton

```markdown
# Title (outcome-oriented, not topic-oriented)

**Owner:** Name
**Status:** Draft | In review | Approved | Archived
**Last updated:** YYYY-MM-DD
**Audience:** Who should read this
**Outcome:** What the reader should know or do after reading

## Summary / TL;DR
## Context
## Body
## Decision / Recommendation (if applicable)
## Open questions
## Next steps (owners + dates)
## References
```

### Memo Checklist

- [ ] One-page or justified length
- [ ] Situation–complication–question–answer structure
- [ ] Recommendation in the first screen
- [ ] Alternatives and trade-offs included
- [ ] Explicit ask of the reader
- [ ] Sent to the smallest audience who must decide

### PRD Checklist

- [ ] User problem with evidence
- [ ] Success metrics defined
- [ ] In-scope / out-of-scope explicit
- [ ] Prioritized requirements (MoSCoW or similar)
- [ ] Dependencies and risks named
- [ ] Launch and support plan linked

### RFC Checklist

- [ ] Summary fits in three sentences
- [ ] At least two alternatives considered
- [ ] Reviewer set and decision deadline named
- [ ] Diagrams for non-trivial designs
- [ ] Rollback/exit plan for irreversible choices
- [ ] Decision recorded in the doc after approval

### SOP Checklist

- [ ] Applicability stated
- [ ] Numbered steps, one action per step
- [ ] Screenshots only where steps are non-obvious
- [ ] Exceptions and escalation paths defined
- [ ] Owner and review cadence visible
- [ ] Tested by someone who did not write it

### Postmortem Checklist

- [ ] Blameless framing confirmed with the team
- [ ] Impact quantified
- [ ] Timeline complete with UTC timestamps
- [ ] Root cause vs. contributing factors distinguished
- [ ] Action items have owners and due dates
- [ ] Internal and external comms drafted

### Email Checklist (for high-stakes messages)

- [ ] Subject line states the ask or decision
- [ ] First paragraph answers: why am I receiving this?
- [ ] Ask bolded or bulleted, with deadline
- [ ] Links to durable docs, not pasted walls of text
- [ ] Correct CC/BCC
- [ ] Legal/HR review if announcing sensitive changes

## Documentation Systems

How teams organize, review, and maintain writing at scale.

### Diátaxis

[Diátaxis](https://diataxis.fr/) is the dominant framework for organizing documentation around four user needs:

| Type | User need | Orientation | Example |
|------|-----------|-------------|---------|
| **Tutorial** | Learning | Learning-oriented | "Get started with X" |
| **How-to guide** | Achieving a goal | Task-oriented | "Deploy to production" |
| **Reference** | Looking up facts | Information-oriented | API parameters, config options |
| **Explanation** | Understanding | Discussion-oriented | "Why we chose event-driven architecture" |

Key rules:
- Do not mix modes on one page (a tutorial that digresses into reference confuses learners).
- Organize site architecture around the four modes.
- Use the Diátaxis compass when deciding where content belongs.

Resources:
- [Diátaxis](https://diataxis.fr/) — the framework
- [Diátaxis application guide](https://diataxis.fr/application/) — how to apply it
- [evildmp/diataxis-documentation-framework](https://github.com/evildmp/diataxis-documentation-framework) — source of the framework

### Docs-as-Code

Treat documentation with the same tools and workflows as software:

- **Plain text** — Markdown, AsciiDoc, reStructuredText.
- **Version control** — Git, same repo as code when possible.
- **Review** — pull requests, CODEOWNERS for docs paths.
- **Automation** — CI builds, link checkers, style linters (Vale, textlint).
- **Publishing** — static site generators or docs platforms as build artifacts.

Resources:
- [Write the Docs — Docs as Code](https://www.writethedocs.org/guide/docs-as-code/) — the classic explainer
- [docs-as-code.org](https://docsas-code.org/) — hitchhiker's guide to documentation as code
- [docToolchain](https://doctoolchain.org/docToolchain/) — open-source docs-as-code toolchain
- *Docs Like Code* — Anne Gentle
- *Modern Technical Writing* — Andrew Etter

### Content Operations

For teams that ship content continuously:

- Editorial calendar with owners and review dates.
- Content inventory and audit cadence (what to keep, update, or retire).
- Style guide + terminology database as the source of truth.
- Review workflow: author → editor → SME → publish.
- Governance: who can publish, who owns which content areas.
- Analytics loop: what content earns its keep.

## Personal Writing Systems

Professionals who write well usually run a personal system behind the scenes.

### Personal Knowledge Management for Writers

- **Capture:** quick inbox for quotes, docs, meeting insights.
- **Organize:** notes linked by project, topic, and person.
- **Distill:** turn raw notes into reusable templates and paragraphs.
- **Express:** ship memos, docs, and posts from the same library.

### Zettelkasten and Notes

- Atomic notes (one idea per note) compose into documents faster.
- Link related notes; serendipity becomes a research method.
- Separate literature notes (sources) from permanent notes (your synthesis).

Tools: [Obsidian](https://obsidian.md/), [Logseq](https://logseq.com/), [Tana](https://tana.inc/), [Heptabase](https://heptabase.com/)

### Template and Snippet Libraries

- Keep a personal (then team) library of: memos, briefs, postmortems, status updates.
- Version them; retire templates that no longer fit how you work.
- Store alongside your style sheet so updates stay in sync.

### Swipe Files

Collect excellent real-world writing:

- Launch emails you admired
- Postmortems published publicly (see [public postmortem collections](https://github.com/ggalihpp/awesome-incident-postmortem))
- Strategy memos from open-source projects
- Policy pages written with unusual clarity

## Building a Writing Practice

- **Set a schedule.** Same time, same place, minimum word or time goal.
- **Track output.** Words shipped, docs published, edits logged — pick a metric.
- **Collect swipe files.** Save excellent memos, docs, and posts you encounter.
- **Write in public occasionally.** External feedback accelerates growth.
- **Find a reader.** A peer or mentor who will tell you the truth about clarity.
- **Specialize then broaden.** Master your job's core genre first, then expand.
- **Study your org's best writers.** Reverse-engineer why their docs travel well.
- **Pair write.** Co-authoring with a stronger writer is the fastest apprenticeship.
- **Keep a "writing retro."** Monthly: what shipped, what stalled, what to change.

### Practice Projects

- Rewrite an old memo as a one-page executive brief.
- Turn a meeting into a pre-read and measure how much shorter it gets.
- Document a process you own so a new hire can run it unaided.
- Draft a "technical explainer" of your work for a non-technical reader.
- Maintain a public changelog of your professional writing for a quarter.
- Volunteer to write the team's next postmortem or launch brief.
- Convert your five most-asked questions into a mini-KB.

## AI-Assisted Professional Writing

AI tools accelerate drafts; they do not own outcomes. Professionals stay accountable for what they publish.

### Where AI Helps

- First drafts from bullet outlines
- Summarizing long threads and docs
- Rewriting for tone or reading level
- Generating alternate headlines and options
- Turning notes into structured outlines
- Editing support: grammar, consistency, terminology checks

### Where Humans Must Own the Work

- Facts, numbers, and citations — always verify
- Judgment calls, recommendations, and trade-offs
- Sensitive, legal, HR, and crisis communications
- Voice quality and organizational nuance
- Final accountability for published content

### Practical Workflow

1. Provide context: audience, purpose, constraints, examples of good output.
2. Request an outline first; critique structure before prose.
3. Draft with AI; rewrite heavily in your voice.
4. Fact-check every claim; link sources.
5. Run through your normal editing passes; AI skips none of them.

### Risks to Manage

- Confidential data leakage into third-party tools — follow company policy.
- Hallucinated facts and citations.
- Homogenized voice that sounds like every other company.
- Over-reliance that atrophies junior writers' craft.

## Editing and Revision

- **Draft, then distance.** Sleep on anything that matters.
- **Edit in passes:** structure → paragraphs → sentences → words → typos.
- **Cut with a reason.** If a line earns its place, keep it; otherwise delete.
- **Kill your darlings.** Clever phrases that slow the reader are liabilities.
- **Use checklists.** Build a personal defect list (hedging, throat-clearing, passive voice).
- **Get specific feedback.** "Is my ask clear?" beats "Thoughts?"
- **Edit for the scan path.** Check headings, first sentences of paragraphs, and bolded text.
- **Verify claims.** Numbers, names, and links get checked before anything else.

### Editing Passes Explained

1. **Structure pass** — Does the order serve the reader? Any section that belongs elsewhere?
2. **Paragraph pass** — One idea per paragraph? Topic sentence clear?
3. **Sentence pass** — Active voice, concrete subjects, no buried verbs.
4. **Word pass** — Cut filler, jargon, and hedging. Prefer short common words.
5. **Mechanics pass** — Grammar, spelling, links, formatting consistency.

### Beta Reading Questions

Ask reviewers these, not "thoughts?":

- What is this document asking you to do?
- Where did you get confused or slow down?
- What would you cut?
- What is missing that you expected to find?
- Would you act on the recommendation as written?

## Common Anti-Patterns

Recognize and remove these defects from your drafts and your team's docs.

### Structural

- **Throat-clearing intros** — "In today's fast-paced environment..." → delete.
- **Buried lede** — the ask appears in paragraph four.
- **Wall of text** — no headings, no lists, no scan path.
- **Document sprawl** — five docs where one decision log would do.
- **Zombie docs** — never updated, still linked, slowly poisoning trust.

### Sentence-level

- **Hedging stacks** — "It might perhaps be possible that we could consider..."
- **Nominalizations** — "We made a recommendation" → "We recommended".
- **Passive evasion** — "Mistakes were made" → name the process failure.
- **Jargon without definition** — internal acronyms in external or cross-team docs.
- **Weasel words** — "many", "some", "industry-leading" without evidence.

### Organizational

- **Meeting as substitute for writing** — if it matters, write it down.
- **Doc by committee without an owner** — consensus prose is usually mush.
- **Rewriting culture without templates** — every person reinvents the memo.
- **Praise for length** — long docs are not automatically thorough.

## Measuring Writing Impact

What gets measured gets improved — cautiously.

### Individual Signals

- Time-to-decision after your memos (should fall).
- Fewer clarifying questions on your docs.
- Peer requests for you to write or review important docs.
- Adoption of your templates by teammates.

### Team Signals

- Incident action-item completion rate (postmortems that change systems).
- Docs freshness: % of pages updated within the last N months.
- Support deflection or ticket volume on documented topics.
- Onboarding time for new hires (docs quality proxy).

### Caution

- Word count and page count are vanity metrics.
- Optimize for decisions made and work unblocked, not pages shipped.

**Specialized Writing**

## Research for Professional Writing

Strong professional writing rests on research, even for short memos.

- **Primary sources first.** Data, customer quotes, and direct observation beat secondhand summaries.
- **Interview stakeholders** with a question list; record decisions, not just opinions.
- **Triangulate.** Two independent sources beat one confident person.
- **Cite for trust.** Link the data source so readers can verify without asking you.
- **Know when research is done.** Diminishing returns arrive quickly for operational docs; do not over-research a status update.

### Research Methods by Document Type

| Document | Minimum research |
|----------|------------------|
| Postmortem | Logs, timeline, participant interviews |
| Strategy memo | Market/customer data, prior decisions, constraints |
| PRD | User research, support tickets, analytics |
| Policy | Legal constraints, comparable policies, failure modes |
| Launch brief | Positioning research, competitive notes, metrics baseline |

## Writing with Data

Every professional eventually writes about numbers. Do it honestly.

- **Lead with the insight, not the chart.** "Revenue churn doubled in Q3" beats "See Figure 1."
- **Give numbers context.** Absolute + relative change, time period, and baseline.
- **Name uncertainty.** Estimates, sample sizes, and confidence ranges belong in the text.
- **Avoid chartjunk and cherry-picking.** If you would be embarrassed by the axis choice in a peer review, fix it.
- **One chart, one message.** Split multi-message visuals into multiple figures.
- **Define metrics inline.** "Activation (first successful job completed within 7 days) rose 12%."

### Writing Numbers Well

- Spell out numbers at sentence start; use digits elsewhere consistently per style guide.
- Use consistent precision: $4.2M, not $4,231,456.73 unless the exact figure matters.
- Round for narrative, provide exact figures in tables or appendices.
- Always answer "so what?" after presenting a number.

Resources:
- *The Wall Street Journal Guide to Business Graphics and Design* — Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic (also *Storytelling with Data*)
- [The Analyst's Style Manual](https://www.analystsmanual.com/) — analytical writing guidance

## Sensitive and High-Stakes Writing

Some documents carry legal, reputational, or human weight. Slow down.

### Incident and Crisis Communications

- Facts first; speculation labeled as such.
- One spokesperson voice; legal/security review before external sends.
- Acknowledge impact and apologize specifically — never boilerplate "we apologize for any inconvenience."
- Timeline of next update, even if there is nothing new.
- Internal comms before or concurrent with external when employees learn from the press.

### Layoffs, Restructures, and Bad News

- Lead with the decision and its human impact; burying the lede destroys trust.
- What is changing, effective when, and what support exists (severance, transitions).
- What is *not* changing — people need stable ground.
- Manager talking points and FAQ so the message does not mutate in delivery.
- Legal review is mandatory; empathy is also mandatory. They are not in conflict.

### Performance and Critical Feedback

- Use SBI: Situation, Behavior, Impact. Avoid character judgments.
- Written feedback should be reviewable by the subject and HR without flinching.
- Document patterns over time; one-off feedback without records helps no one.
- Separate developmental feedback from evaluation feedback when possible.

### Legal and Compliance-Sensitive Writing

- Never improvise legal interpretations in email; route through counsel.
- "Privileged and confidential" labels only when counsel advises — casual labeling trains people to ignore labels.
- Retention: assume everything you write may be discoverable.
- When in doubt, write less, ask more.

## Career Writing

Your career is partly a portfolio of documents you may not think of as writing.

### Résumés and Cover Letters

- One page early career; two pages max for most professionals.
- Bullets start with strong verbs and end with quantified outcomes.
- Mirror keywords from the job description honestly.
- Cover letter: why this company, why you, one proof point each.

### Performance Self-Reviews and Promo Packets

- Collect wins monthly; do not reconstruct a year from memory.
- Structure: scope growth, impact with metrics, peer/manager quotes, leadership behaviors.
- Write for the calibration committee who does not know your work.
- Map achievements to the level criteria explicitly.

### LinkedIn and Professional Presence

- Headline = role + specialty + proof, not just title.
- Featured section holds your best public writing and talks.
- Posts that teach something from your work outperform generic inspiration.

### Letters of Recommendation and References

- Ask for the job description and draft bullet points for the recommender.
- Specific anecdotes beat adjectives.
- Give recommenders at least two weeks.

## Voice, Tone, and Content Systems

Individual documents inherit from systems. Build or adopt one.

### Voice vs. Tone

- **Voice** = the stable personality of the writer or organization (rarely changes).
- **Tone** = the situational register (changes by context: error message vs. launch post).

### Building a Voice Guide

1. Describe voice in 3–5 adjectives with "we are / we are not" pairs.
2. Provide do/don't word lists for brand terms, competitor terms, and sensitive topics.
3. Show examples rewritten in voice for common document types.
4. Define tone shifts for: errors, empty states, pricing, apologies, legal notices.
5. Version the guide and assign an owner.

### Content Design (User-Need First)

Popularized by GOV.UK: start from the user need, not the org chart.

- Research what users actually search for and struggle with.
- Design content types and templates for repeated needs.
- Measure success by task completion, not publishing volume.
- Ruthlessly retire content that no longer serves a need.

## Plain Language, Accessibility, and Inclusive Writing

Plain language is not dumbing down; it is respect for the reader's time and ability.

### Plain Language Principles

- Use everyday words; define necessary jargon once.
- Active voice, present tense, direct address ("you", "we").
- Short sentences (aim ~15–20 words average); one idea per sentence.
- Front-load the main point; logical headings that describe content.
- Lists and tables over dense paragraphs.
- Test readability; aim for the audience you actually have (often grade 8–10 for public content).

Resources:
- [plainlanguage.gov](https://www.plainlanguage.gov/guidelines/) — U.S. federal plain language guidelines
- [Federal Plain Language Guidelines (PDF)](https://www.plainlanguage.gov/media/FederalPLGuidelines.pdf)
- [EU Plain language guidelines](https://op.europa.eu/documents/d/accessibility/accessible-publishing-plain-language-guidelines)

### Accessibility in Writing

- Meaningful link text ("Read the incident report") not "click here."
- Headings are real headings, not bold text (screen readers navigate by heading level).
- Write alt text that conveys the information in images and charts.
- Avoid directional language as the only cue ("the button on the right").
- Prefer "select" over "click"; "enter" over "type" when input methods vary.
- People-first language by default; follow community preference for identity-first terms.

Resources:
- [Microsoft Style Guide — Writing for all abilities](https://learn.microsoft.com/style-guide/accessibility/writing-all-abilities)
- [W3C WAI — Clear words](https://www.w3.org/WAI/GL/WCAG3/2020/how-tos/clear-words/)
- [Google Tech Writing for Accessibility](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/accessibility)
- [18F — Accessibility for content](https://accessibility.18f.gov/)

### Inclusive Writing

- Avoid idioms and cultural metaphors in global docs.
- Spell out acronyms on first use; do not assume shared slang.
- Use gender-neutral language ("they", "chairperson", "workforce").
- Review for bias in examples, stock scenarios, and role names.
- When describing people, avoid othering framings ("the disabled" → "disabled people" or "people with disabilities" per audience preference).
